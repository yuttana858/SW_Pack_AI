# Cushion design

## Overview: the cushion design problem

A cushion protects a product by spreading a drop impact over a longer stopping distance and time, so the product's peak deceleration stays below the level that damages it. Cushion design needs four inputs:

1. **Product fragility** — the peak deceleration (G) the product survives, from a damage-boundary test (ASTM D3332) [S1].
2. **Design drop height** — from the distribution environment, package weight and handling [S6][S7].
3. **Cushion material data** — cushion curves (peak G vs static stress) for the chosen thickness and drop height, from ASTM D1596 testing [S11][S13].
4. **Product weight and bearing area** — to set static stress.

The design also has to check vibration (natural frequency vs transport vibration), creep under long-term static load, multiple impacts and temperature. Every number in this file is industry-typical or illustrative unless marked otherwise — *replace with supplier/house data in `company/`*.

## Product fragility, G factor and the damage boundary curve (ASTM D3332)

**Fragility** is a product property, like size or weight: the largest shock input the product tolerates without functional damage. It is usually expressed as peak acceleration in multiples of standard gravity (G). Fragility is the reciprocal of ruggedness [S2].

**ASTM D3332** (D3332-99, reapproved 2023) measures fragility on a programmable shock machine in two steps [S1]:

- **Method A — critical velocity change (ΔVc).** Short shock pulses (duration 3 ms or less) with increasing velocity change find the velocity change below which damage does not occur regardless of peak acceleration.
- **Method B — critical acceleration (Ac).** Trapezoidal pulses with a velocity change of at least 1.57 × ΔVc and increasing peak acceleration find the acceleration above which damage occurs.

Plotted as peak acceleration vs velocity change, the two results form the **damage boundary curve**: damage occurs only when both ΔV > ΔVc and peak G > Ac. Each product has separate critical values for each axis and direction tested [S1].

**Using it in design.** If the product's ΔVc is above the velocity change of the worst expected drop, no cushion is needed for that axis. Otherwise, the cushion must keep peak G below Ac. Westpak notes that the method is conservative: the square-wave input excites every product resonance at its maximum, which rarely happens in service, and prototype samples often give lower values than production units [S2].

## Typical fragility levels (for early estimates only)

Published fragility bands (use only when no D3332 data exist):

| Category | Fragility, G | Examples given by the source |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely fragile | 15–25 | Precision instruments, early hard drives [S3][S4] |
| Very delicate | 25–40 | Digital electronics (hard drives) [S3] |
| Delicate | 40–60 | Computer display terminals, printers, office equipment [S3] |
| Moderately delicate | 60–85 | Televisions, video equipment [S3]; TVs, appliances, printers [S4] |
| Moderately rugged | 85–115 | Major appliances, furniture [S3] |

**VERIFY — conflicts.** The same source lists hard drives in both the 15–25 G and 25–40 G bands [S3]; another places printers at 60–85 G rather than 40–60 G [S4]. One published case study calculates a fragility of 70 G for a 20 kg flat-panel TV [S5]. A product with fragility below about 30 G is a candidate for redesign of the product itself rather than more cushioning [S4]. Measure fragility with ASTM D3332 for any real design.

## Design drop height vs package weight

Drop height falls as package weight rises, because heavy packages are carried lower and are not thrown. Field data often show a near-linear relation between drop height and package weight [S10]. MIL-HDBK-304 sets drop height by size, weight and level of protection, and notes that hand-holds lower the drop height [S7].

**Commercial probable drop heights (PadCAD, Canadian Conservation Institute)** — described as an estimate of infrequent, worst-case drops in typical commercial shipping [S6]:

| Package mass | Handling | Drop height |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 kg [0–10 lb] | One person, thrown | 1 210 mm [48 in] |
| 6–9 kg [11–20 lb] | One person, thrown | 1 070 mm [42 in] |
| 9–23 kg [20–50 lb] | One person, carried | 910 mm [36 in] |
| 23–113 kg [50–250 lb] | Two persons, carried | 760 mm [30 in] |

**Other published values (VERIFY — they differ):**
- A case manufacturer gives about 30 in (760 mm) for one-person carry under 20 lb (9 kg), 24–30 in (610–760 mm) for 21–50 lb (9.5–23 kg) and 18–24 in (460–610 mm) for two-person carry of 51–100 lb (23–45 kg) [S8].
- Westpak's review of the FPL-22 field study takes a two-person carry as 30 in (760 mm); only 1 % of drops exceeded 58 in (1 470 mm) for a 25 lb (11.3 kg) container and 30 in (760 mm) for a 43 lb (19.5 kg) container [S9].
- MIL-HDBK-304 cushion data cover drop heights of 12–48 in (305–1 220 mm) in 6 in (152 mm) steps [S7].

For test validation, drop heights come from the chosen test procedure (ISTA or ASTM D4169; see `testing/distribution-testing.md`).

## Free-fall impact velocity

```
v = sqrt(2 × g × h)
```

- `v` — impact velocity, m/s [in/s]
- `g` — 9.80665 m/s² [386.09 in/s²]
- `h` — free-fall drop height, m [in]

**Validity:** free fall from rest, air drag negligible (true for package drops up to a few metres). **Limitations:** ignores rotation and the stroke of the cushion; real drops onto edges and corners transfer less energy to the flat-face cushion.

**Worked example (SI):** h = 0.914 m → v = sqrt(2 × 9.80665 × 0.914) = **4.23 m/s**.
**Worked example (US):** h = 36 in → v = sqrt(2 × 386.09 × 36) = **166.7 in/s** (13.9 ft/s).

## Shock formulas: velocity change, half-sine pulse and ideal cushion

**Velocity change with rebound**

```
ΔV = (1 + e) × v
```

- `ΔV` — velocity change during impact, m/s [in/s]
- `e` — coefficient of restitution, 0 (no rebound) to 1 (perfect rebound), –

**Half-sine shock pulse (relation between peak G, ΔV and duration)**

```
G_peak = (π × ΔV) / (2 × g × τ)
```

- `G_peak` — peak acceleration, in g (dimensionless multiple of g)
- `τ` — pulse duration, s

Validity: an idealised half-sine pulse. Cushioned impacts are roughly half-sine; very stiff impacts approach a square or triangular pulse.

**Worked example (SI):** ΔV = 4.23 m/s (no rebound), τ = 10 ms → G = π × 4.23 / (2 × 9.80665 × 0.010) = **67.8 G**.
**Worked example (US):** ΔV = 166.7 in/s, τ = 0.010 s → G = π × 166.7 / (2 × 386.09 × 0.010) = **67.8 G**.

**Ideal cushion (energy balance)** — a cushion that applies a constant force over its full thickness absorbs the drop energy `m g h` over stroke `t`, so:

```
G_ideal ≈ h / t        and        t_min ≈ h / G_f
```

- `t` — cushion thickness used as stopping stroke, mm [in]
- `G_f` — product fragility, G

Validity: theoretical lower bound. Real foams cannot use their full thickness (they bottom out at high strain) and do not apply constant force, so the real thickness needed is larger; the cushion curve gives the real value. This is a derived relation, not a design value.

**Worked example:** h = 914 mm [36 in], G_f = 50 → t_min ≈ 914 / 50 = **18.3 mm** [36 / 50 = **0.72 in**]. A real foam cushion will need more.

## Cushion curves (ASTM D1596)

A **cushion curve** plots peak deceleration transmitted (G) against **static stress** for one material, one thickness and one drop height [S11]. ASTM D1596 is the test that generates them by dropping weighted platens onto cushion samples over a range of static loads [S13]. Curves are usually published for the first impact and for the average of impacts 2–5.

Shape and reading:
- At **low static stress** the cushion is too stiff for the load: it barely compresses and G is high.
- At **high static stress** the cushion bottoms out: G rises steeply.
- Between them is a **minimum** — the best operating range for that thickness and drop height.
- Thicker cushions give lower curves; higher drop heights give higher curves.

**Static stress**

```
σ_s = W / A = (m × g) / A
```

- `σ_s` — static stress, kPa [psi]
- `W` — product weight, N [lbf]; `m` — product mass, kg [lb]
- `A` — cushion bearing area under the product, m² [in²]

Most common polymer foam cushions work best between about 0.5 and 2.0 psi (3.4–13.8 kPa) static stress [S12]. Example from the same source: a 47 lb (21.3 kg) product at 0.75 psi (5.2 kPa) needs about 63 in² (406 cm²) of bearing area [S12].

Generating cushion curves by D1596 needs a lot of test data and is time-consuming and expensive [S11], so use the supplier's published curves for the exact grade, density and thickness.

## Cushion design procedure (step by step)

1. **Define the product:** mass, dimensions, centre of gravity, flat bearing surfaces, fragility per axis (ASTM D3332) [S1].
2. **Choose the design drop height** from the distribution environment or the test procedure the package must pass (see drop-height table above).
3. **Estimate the minimum thickness** with `t_min ≈ h / G_f` as a sanity check.
4. **Select candidate materials** (see materials section) and obtain their cushion curves at your drop height.
5. **For each thickness curve, find the range of static stress where peak G < G_f** (with a margin, e.g. 10–20 % — a house decision, not a standard value).
6. **Choose the thinnest cushion** whose curve gives acceptable G, and pick static stress near the curve minimum.
7. **Compute bearing area** `A = W / σ_s` for each face; design pads, ribs or end caps to give that area.
8. **Check buckling:** keep cushion pads stable (a common rule is minimum bearing area relative to thickness so the pad does not buckle — check supplier guidance).
9. **Check creep** at the chosen static stress (ASTM D2221) and add thickness if the cushion will be loaded for long periods [S19].
10. **Check vibration:** estimate the natural frequency of product-on-cushion and compare with the transport PSD.
11. **Check temperature** extremes on the route; use curves measured at those temperatures.
12. **Prototype and validate** with the chosen distribution test (ISTA / ASTM D4169).

## Worked cushion design example (SI and US)

**Inputs.** Product mass 12 kg [26.46 lb]; fragility 50 G (assumed from a D3332 test); design drop height from PadCAD for 9–23 kg one-person carry: 91 cm [36 in] [S6]; the calculation uses 36 in = 914 mm.

**Step 1 — impact velocity:** v = sqrt(2 × 9.80665 × 0.914) = 4.23 m/s [166.7 in/s].

**Step 2 — theoretical minimum thickness:** t_min = 914 / 50 = 18.3 mm [0.72 in].

**Step 3 — read the cushion curve (ILLUSTRATIVE).** *The following curve reading is hypothetical and is not data for any real material.* Suppose the supplier's curve for a chosen foam at 910 mm [36 in] drop shows that a 50 mm [2 in] thickness keeps peak G below 50 for static stresses around 5.2 kPa [0.75 psi], with its minimum near that value. Choose σ_s = 5.17 kPa [0.75 psi].

**Step 4 — bearing area per loaded face:**
- SI: W = 12 × 9.80665 = 117.7 N; A = 117.7 / 5 171 Pa = 0.02276 m² = **227.6 cm²**.
- US: A = 26.46 lb / 0.75 psi = **35.3 in²** (= 227.6 cm²).

**Step 5 — design:** e.g. four corner pads of about 57 cm² [8.8 in²] each per face, each 50 mm [2 in] thick, checked for buckling and creep, then validated by drop testing.

Limitations: the result depends entirely on the real cushion curve; the multi-drop (2–5) curve should be used if the package will see repeated drops; corners and edges need separate checks because their bearing area differs.

## Creep and long-term static load

Under a constant static load, foam cushions slowly lose thickness (creep). Lost thickness means less stroke for the next drop and can leave slack between product and cushion, letting the product move [S19].

- **ASTM D2221** measures creep of package cushioning materials; results depend on static load, specimen area, shape and thickness, and on temperature and humidity [S19].
- **ISO 10066** measures creep in compression of flexible cellular materials over 72 h; the allowable creep and safe static stress depend on cushion thickness, package life and storage conditions [S20].

Design rule: if the package will be stored or shipped loaded for weeks, choose static stress from the creep data, not only from the cushion curve minimum, and allow for the thickness loss.

## Multiple impacts

Cushion curves are traditionally published for the first impact, but the mechanical properties of closed-cell foams keep changing with later impacts [S21]. Use curves for the average of drops 2–5 when the package will see repeated drops (all ISTA and ASTM D4169 sequences include several drops). Single-impact materials such as EPS lose much of their performance after the first crush [S18]; resilient foams such as PE and EPP recover better [S14][S15].

## Temperature effects

Foam stiffness changes with temperature: in general foams get stiffer when cold and softer when hot, which moves the cushion curve. ASTM D2221 lists temperature and humidity as factors that affect creep results [S19]. No quantitative temperature correction was confirmed in this pass — **VERIFY**: ask the supplier for cushion curves measured at the route's minimum and maximum temperatures (for example, cold-chain or air-cargo holds) and design to the worst case.

## Vibration isolation: natural frequency and transmissibility

A product resting on a cushion behaves like a mass on a spring. The natural frequency is:

```
f_n = (1 / (2π)) × sqrt(k / m) = (1 / (2π)) × sqrt(g / δ_st)
```

- `f_n` — natural frequency, Hz
- `k` — cushion stiffness, N/m [lbf/in]; for a foam pad `k ≈ E × A / t` (E = compressive modulus, A = area, t = thickness)
- `m` — product mass, kg [in US units use weight/g with g = 386.09 in/s²]
- `δ_st` — static deflection of the cushion under the product's weight, m [in]

**Transmissibility** (ratio of product response to input) for a damped single-degree-of-freedom system:

```
T = sqrt( (1 + (2ζr)²) / ((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) ),   r = f / f_n
```

- `ζ` — damping ratio, –; `r` — frequency ratio, –

Behaviour (derived from the equation and checked numerically):
- At r = 1 (resonance) T is at its maximum: about 5.1 for ζ = 0.1 and 1.9 for ζ = 0.3. Peak transmissibility, and most vibration damage, occurs at the natural frequency [S22].
- At r = √2 ≈ 1.414, T = 1 for any damping; above this the cushion **isolates** (T < 1).
- At r = 2, T ≈ 0.36 (ζ = 0.1); at r = 3, T ≈ 0.15 (ζ = 0.1).

So a cushion can do nothing, amplify, or isolate vibration depending on thickness, bearing area and forcing frequency. Design so that f_n is well below the dominant input frequencies, or at least away from the PSD peaks of the transport mode (see `testing/distribution-testing.md`).

**Worked example (SI).** m = 12 kg on cushion pads with total stiffness k = 50 kN/m: f_n = (1/2π) × sqrt(50 000 / 12) = **10.3 Hz**. Static deflection δ_st = 117.7 N / 50 000 N/m = 2.35 mm. Isolation begins above 1.414 × 10.3 = **14.5 Hz**.

**Worked example (US).** W = 26.46 lb, k = 285.5 lbf/in: δ_st = 26.46 / 285.5 = 0.0927 in; f_n = (1/2π) × sqrt(386.09 / 0.0927) = **10.3 Hz**.

**Validity and limitations:** linear spring, single axis, small amplitude. Foam is non-linear and its stiffness and damping change with static stress, amplitude and temperature; cushion curves alone cannot accurately define natural frequency or transmissibility [S22]. Use supplier vibration-transmissibility curves (or a sine-sweep test) for the actual material and static stress.

## Summary checklist for cushion design reviews

- Fragility measured (D3332) per axis, not taken from a table.
- Drop height matches the test procedure the package must pass.
- Cushion curve used is for the right material, density, thickness, drop height and drop number (1st vs 2–5).
- Static stress is near the curve minimum and within creep limits.
- Bearing area achieved on every face, edge and corner; pads checked for buckling.
- Natural frequency kept away from transport PSD peaks.
- Temperature extremes considered.
- Design confirmed by distribution testing.
