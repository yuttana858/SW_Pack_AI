# Corrugator process, converting equipment and quality defects

> Confidence: **industry-typical**. Process settings are machine- and mill-specific. Replace with house data in `company/`.
> Source note: sources were consulted through search-indexed extracts, and several primary TAPPI documents are paywalled. The TAPPI *Corrugating Defect/Remedy Manual* [S22] and TIP 0304-32 [S23] are the reference texts for detailed remedies.

## Corrugator process overview

The wet end makes single-face web. The double backer adds the second liner. The dry end slits, scores and cuts the board into sheets.

1. **Roll stands and splicers**: feed the liner and medium reels continuously.
2. **Preheaters**: heated drums raise the web temperature before bonding. The wrap angle around the drum controls how much heat goes in [S7].
3. **Medium conditioning**: the medium is brought to the right moisture before fluting [S3].
4. **Single facer**:
   - Corrugating rolls form the flutes in the medium.
   - Starch is applied to the flute tips.
   - A pressure roll presses the liner onto the fluted medium [S3][S5].
5. **Bridge**: single-face web is stored in festoons. This lets the double backer run at a different speed from the single facer, especially during reel or order changes [S3].
6. **Glue machine and double backer**:
   - Starch is applied to the flute tips of the single-face web, and the outer (double-back) liner is added.
   - The board then passes over hot plates.
   - Bonding pressure here is much lower than at the single facer, so the starch needs several seconds of heat to cure. The double backer has long been the limit on corrugator speed [S3].
7. **Dry end**:
   - The slitter-scorer applies the slits and the flap scores. Scoring heads run against anvils (see `manufacturing/die-cutting-and-tooling.md`).
   - A rotary knife (shear) cuts the web across into sheets [S3].
   - Sheets are stacked so that fibres can dry and the adhesive can cure [S3].

**Take-up factor**: the length of medium used per length of board. FEFCO gives about 1.50–1.55 for A flute, 1.30–1.35 for B, 1.40–1.45 for C and 1.20–1.35 for E [S1]. A typical C-flute value of 1.43 means 1.43 m² of medium makes 1 m² of board [S1]. Worked example: 815.5 m² of board (1000 RSCs, see `dimensional/blank-size-formulas.md`) × 1.43 = **1166 m²** of C-flute medium [12 553 ft²].

## Starch adhesive (Stein-Hall type)

- Corrugating adhesive is usually a two-part Stein-Hall system:
  - a cooked **carrier** starch, which provides viscosity and suspension;
  - raw (uncooked) starch, which carries the bonding.
  
  One patent describes the cooked part as about 15–17 % of total starch (**VERIFY** against the adhesive supplier) [S4].
- At the **gel point** the raw starch gelatinises with heat. Viscosity rises immediately and the green bond forms [S4].
- Gel temperature, viscosity and solids are the key control parameters. Getting them wrong causes high-low flutes, poor bonds and edge problems [S21].

## Warp: causes and fixes

**Definition**: warp is board that is not flat after the corrugator. It shows as up-warp, down-warp, S-warp or twist. FEFCO measures it as rise divided by span, in percent (see `dimensional/tolerances.md`).

| Cause | Mechanism | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture imbalance between the liners | the wetter or hotter side expands, so the board curls away from it [S6][S9] | balance the heat and moisture input on the two liners |
| Excess starch on one side | adds water to that side [S6] | reduce the glue gap or application. If single-facer starch is cut, cut double-backer starch to match [S6] |
| Preheater wrap mismatch | the liner wrapped more than the medium enters bonding hotter [S7] | adjust wrap arms on the top and bottom liners |
| Too little heat or too much moisture on the top layer | top layer expands, giving down-curl [S7] | increase top-liner preheat |
| Speed changes at the double backer | heat exposure changes with speed | lift the weight rolls with speed to keep heat input constant [S6] |

- Adding preheat or glue before the double backer cures some warp but costs money. Extra heat can also cause cracking at the die cutters [S8].
- The traditional manual method is to inspect the board, identify the warp direction, and add heat to the liner on the correct side [S9].
- **Downstream impact**: digital presses are reported to tolerate no more than 1/8 in of warp per linear foot (~10 mm/m) [S26]. Feeders on flexo folder-gluers and die cutters also jam with warped board (machine limits are house or OEM data).

## Converting equipment

| Equipment | What it does | Typical products | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexo folder-gluer (FFG) | prints (flexo post-print), slots, scores, glues or stitches the manufacturer's joint, counts and bundles | RSC, HSC, OSC, FOL | most common RSC line. Feed rolls and print stations can crush board [S10] |
| Rotary die cutter (RDC) | prints in line, then die-cuts on a cylinder against an anvil | high-volume die-cut trays and mailers | fast continuous motion. Tooling costs more [S29] |
| Flatbed die cutter | platen die-cutting with stripping and blanking | complex die-cuts, litho-laminated displays | slower stop–start action, high precision [S29] |
| Specialty folder-gluer | folds and glues die-cut blanks: straight-line, crash-lock (auto-bottom, e.g. FEFCO 0711), 4- and 6-corner trays | auto-bottom boxes, trays, mailers | crash-lock modules pre-glue the bottom flaps (**VERIFY** machine capability) |
| Litho-lamination | an offset-printed liner or sheet is laminated onto single-face or corrugated board | premium retail and display | photographic quality. Cost rises above about 28 × 40 in sheet size [S24] |
| Pre-print (flexo on reel) | the liner is printed on reel before the corrugator | high volume, medium-to-high resolution | the print must survive corrugator heat [S24] |
| Digital (inkjet, post-print) | prints directly on corrugated sheets | short runs, versioning | no plates. Shorter lead time than lamination, which can take 8–12 weeks [S25]. Warp sensitive [S26] |

## Process effects on strength

- **Caliper loss (crush)**:
  - Converting should lose no more than about 0.08 mm [0.003 in] of caliper in non-printed areas [S10].
  - Over-crush in feed sections and print stations is common, and mills then specify heavier liners to compensate [S10].
  - Caliper loss lowers bending stiffness, and with it box compression (the McKee relation uses ECT and caliper) [S30]. See `performance/`.
- **ECT loss in converting**: one industry analysis reports that converting variation can destroy up to 35 % of a structure's ECT [S11].
- **Print impression**:
  - Extra print impression on recycled board, used for smooth ink laydown, adds crush.
  - Plates softer than the common 32 Shore A have measured less post-print crush [S12].
- **Score cracking**:
  - The paper property most involved is stretch, which depends partly on moisture [S14].
  - In one study, linerboard moisture fell from about 7 % to about 4 % at 25 %RH, and strain fell by 11 % [S15].
  - Recycled liners are more sensitive, and so is board overheated on the corrugator [S13][S14].
- **Moisture in general**: board strength falls as humidity rises (see `performance/` environmental factors). Moisture control is a balance between cracking (too dry) and warp or low stiffness (too wet).

## Quality defects: causes and remedies

| Defect | Description | Typical causes | Remedies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washboarding | wave-like ripple of the liner over the flutes [S17] | the glue line shrinks as it dries. Ripple depth rises with glue applied and with humidity [S19]. Low-grammage or weak liner is more prone [S19] | reduce starch application. Use stiffer liner, or a multi-ply liner with an absorbent inner ply [S20] |
| Warp | board not flat | heat or moisture imbalance, starch imbalance [S6][S7] | see the warp table above |
| High-low flutes | uneven flute heights, giving uneven caliper [S17] | uneven, dry or cool glue at the edges; viscosity too high or too low; wrong gel temperature; vacuum failure at the single facer [S21] | correct the adhesive, the heat profile across the web, and single-facer vacuum and pressure [S21][S22] |
| Blisters / loose edges | local unbonded areas | low green-bond strength; bond separates under stress [S23] | check gel point, glue film, heat and pressure [S23] |
| Delamination | liner separates from the medium [S17] | heat-transfer problems [S18]; adhesive or paper problems [S23] | the paper, equipment, adhesive and heat must all be checked together [S23] |
| Crushed board | flutes compressed, lower caliper and cushioning [S17] | over-pressure in feed rolls, print or die cutter; nip settings [S10] | set nips to measured caliper. Keep caliper loss ≤ ~0.08 mm [S10] |
| Score cracks | the liner cracks along the fold | low moisture or stretch, overheating, recycled or dense liners, worn or wrong score profile [S13][S14] | target 5.8–7.5 % total board moisture [S13]. Humidify (one case cut cracking from ~10 % to ≤1 %) [S16]. Keep press run-out in check and change the score profile [S14] |
| Fishtail (skew) at the joint | gap at the top differs from the gap at the bottom | folder misalignment, uneven feed, warp | keep within ≤ 3/16 in measured at the flap scores (PMMI via AICC) [S27] |
| Gap out of limit | joint gap too small (overlap, binding) or too large | panel sizes, folding-belt setting | 1/8–1/2 in, target 3/8 in [S27] |

- Lighter flutes and media may need lower steam temperatures in some corrugator sections to avoid heat defects [S18].
- For exhaustive cause–remedy trees, use the TAPPI *Corrugating Defect/Remedy Manual* [S22].

## Flute geometry reference for process settings

Nominal flute thickness is E 1.6 mm [1/16 in], B 3.2 mm [1/8 in], C 4.0 mm [5/32 in] and A 4.8 mm [3/16 in] [S28]. FEFCO quotes different flute heights, for example B 2.4 mm and C 3.6 mm [S1] (**VERIFY**: height and thickness are measured differently). Use measured combined caliper for converting set-up, and set nips and gaps from it, not from the nominal value.
